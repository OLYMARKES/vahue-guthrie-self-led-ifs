July 29 alignment call

Included

OS 1.0 → OS 2.0, attention as focus, awareness as field, access to Self as a continuum, and the ground–core–field–focus sequence.

Source: call transcript, voicebank and approved working direction
Open full candidate material
A wider operating system

Most of us have learned to meet life through focused attention: analysing, planning, naming and trying to solve one thing at a time. Guthrie calls this OS 1.0. It is useful, but it can become overconcentrated and effortful when we rely on it alone.

OS 2.0 adds awareness. Attention can still focus, but it is held inside a wider field that also includes the body, the room, other people and what matters most. The point is not to discard thinking. It is to transcend and include it: focus remains available without having to carry the whole experience.

The direction of the course

This course is not asking participants to create a rare or permanent state. It is helping them notice a capacity that is already present, bring it from the background into the foreground, and gradually increase both the frequency and the duration of access to Self.

When access becomes shallower, parts language offers a simple doorway. “I am rushing” can become “A part of me is rushing.” The situation has not disappeared, but even a small amount of space may return. From there, the participant can relate to the experience rather than be completely defined by it.

Possible placement: Lesson 1 as the course frame, or Lesson 2 as the bridge from attention to awareness.

Guthrie’s presencing inductions

Partly included

The seven-minute core practice is adapted from Guthrie’s recurring live-call openings. The source can also support a short approved audio script.

Source: presencing_inductions_guthrie.md
Open full candidate material
Candidate seven-minute practice
  1. Arrive. Find an upright, comfortable position. Let the spine support you without becoming rigid. Eyes may close or rest in a soft gaze.
  2. Ground. Feel the feet and the weight of the body meeting the seat. Let gravity carry what it can carry. Notice any sense of stability or stillness.
  3. Core. Bring light attention to the torso: belly, chest, back and throat. Feel the body from within without changing what you find.
  4. Field. Allow awareness to open into the room. Include sound, peripheral vision and the body together. Thoughts and sensations may come and go inside this wider field.
  5. Focus. Let attention rest lightly on one simple thing while ground, core and field remain available. Focus does not need to collapse the wider awareness.
  6. Return. Notice how much access to Self may be present: none you can identify, a little, or more. Complete the sentence, “Access to Self may feel like…”

This could become a Guthrie-recorded audio with transcript and captions. The final language should preserve his pacing and be approved before publication.

Safety boundary: every invitation is optional. If there is strain or disorientation, open the eyes, orient to the room and stop.

Self — ORIGINAL

Could add a little more theory: Self as innate and undamaged, Self as the seat of awareness, selected 8 Cs, and markers of access. The main decision is how much belongs in Week 1 without turning it into a theory lecture.

Source: old course Week 4 handout · adapt, do not reproduce wholesale
Open full candidate material
What IFS means by Self

IFS proposes that Self is not another part and is not something a person has to manufacture. It is an innate capacity for awareness and relationship. It may be obscured when protective parts are active, but in the IFS model it is not damaged or lost.

Self can be understood as the seat of awareness: the capacity that can notice, hold and understand experience. Parts are contents of consciousness; Self is the awareness able to be in relationship with them.

How access may be recognised

The familiar eight Cs — calm, clarity, curiosity, compassion, confidence, courage, creativity and connectedness — are signals, not a checklist participants must perform. Access may also show up more quietly: a little more space, an easier breath, less urgency, softer focus, or the ability to stay curious without forcing an outcome.

Self-leadership does not mean getting rid of parts. It means that parts can be seen and valued without having to run the whole system. The relationship changes before the experience necessarily changes.

Week 1 could use the first two paragraphs and a short marker list. The fuller 8 Cs and Self-leadership teaching could be developed later.

The Six Organizing Principles of IFS

Select only

The previous course’s Week 1 handout may supply one clear systems paragraph or visual map. It should not be compressed into the participant lesson as a protocol.

Source: old Week 1 handout in English and Russian
Open full candidate material
A light systems frame for participants
  1. We have many parts. Different inner voices, impulses and positions are a natural feature of the mind.
  2. Parts are not bad. Even when their behaviour creates difficulty, they usually carry an understandable protective intention.
  3. Parts live in relationship. They form patterns, react to one another and try to keep the system safe or balanced.
  4. We are more than our parts. IFS calls the aware, relational centre of the system Self.
  5. Self is the guiding direction. More access to Self makes curiosity, clarity and compassionate leadership more available.
  6. Trust the pace of the system. Change is invited through relationship and respect rather than force.

For Week 1, these principles can function as an orientation map. They explain why the course uses parts language and why the participant is never asked to battle, suppress or diagnose an inner experience.

This adaptation deliberately removes practitioner instructions, client language and advanced material about exile work or unburdening.

Awareness experiments and release points

Placed by stage

The bowl-of-soup experiment belongs in the Week 1 live session with debrief. Walking and peripheral-vision practice appear only after Guthrie introduces them. The fingers variation is held back.

Source: July 29 call exercise bank
Open full candidate material
1. Peripheral vision · after live, if introduced

Soften the eyes and notice how much of the room can be included without strain. Compare a calm moment with an ordinary busy moment. The earlier fingers variation is not proposed for this course.

2. Three channels while walking · after live

In an ordinary, safe place, walk first with attention on seeing. Then let hearing become more prominent. Then notice sensations in the body. For the final minute, see whether all three channels can be present together without forcing equal attention.

3. The bowl-of-soup test · live session only

Imagine carrying a full bowl across a room, or try the experiment with an unbreakable cup in a safe environment. First notice what happens when attention clamps tightly onto the object. Then find ground, widen the field and allow a light focus on the object. Compare effort, stability and available awareness.

Release rule: do not publish an experiment as independent participant practice until Guthrie has introduced it live. These are observations, not tests to pass.

Week 2 · Parts language

Parts are people, not symptoms

Included

The strongest local source for Week 2: parts as distinct inner people with their own perspectives, needs, fears, histories, strategies and gifts.

Source: Guthrie Sayen, “Parts Are People” · previous-course lesson and Week 3 transcript · adaptation approval pending
Open adapted teaching material
Multiplicity is ordinary

A person can genuinely want to rest and genuinely want to finish the work. One part can welcome a new relationship while another expects rejection. IFS treats these differences as the normal multiplicity of mind, not as evidence that a person is broken.

What makes a part more than a symptom

A part may have a characteristic age, tone, posture, body sensation, belief, hope, fear and strategy. It may remember the world from the time when its role first became necessary. Meeting it as an inner person invites relationship; reducing it to “my anxiety” or “my procrastination” can hide what it is trying to accomplish.

Strategy and intention are different

A strategy may be costly while the intention beneath it is protective. A critic may try to prevent humiliation. A perfectionist may try to secure belonging. A pleasing part may preserve attachment. An angry part may defend a boundary. An avoidant part may prevent overwhelm.

Blending and unblending

When a part blends, its view can feel like the whole truth: “I am a failure,” “I must fix this now,” or “Nothing matters.” Unblending does not banish the part. It creates enough space to say, “A part of me believes this,” so Self and the part can begin to know one another.

Participant boundary: use this for self-observation only. The Six Fs and practitioner-led unblending protocol remain outside this course.

Parts form a system

Included

A light systems layer: parts react to one another, become polarised, and may carry beliefs or emotions that are not their essential nature.

Sources: IFS Institute, “The Internal Family Systems Model Outline” and “IFS Glossary of Terms”
Open adapted teaching material
Inner sequences

Parts rarely act alone. A demanding part may push harder when a tired part withdraws; the tired part may withdraw further when it feels attacked. The problem is not either part by itself but the escalating relationship between them.

Polarisation

Polarised parts take increasingly opposite positions because each fears what would happen if the other prevailed. “Work without stopping” and “refuse to begin” may both be attempts to keep the person from shame, failure or depletion.

Burdens

IFS uses burden for an extreme belief, emotion or role carried by a part but not understood as its essential nature. This course introduces the word so participants can follow IFS language; it does not teach an unburdening process.

Examples and reflection options

Optional depth

Additional participant material for readers who want more examples without moving into practitioner technique.

Sources: old-course handout and transcript; anonymised themes from course custdev; Richard Schwartz, “Introduction to IFS” and “No Bad Parts”
Open full candidate material
A one-situation map
  1. Name one ordinary current situation.
  2. List up to three different inner positions without deciding which is right.
  3. For each, complete: “This part seems to want…” and “This part may fear…”
  4. Notice whether any part objects to the exercise. That objection also belongs on the map.
A language ladder

Move from identity to relationship: “I am anxious” → “Something in me is anxious” → “A part of me is anxious” → “I notice a part that expects something difficult.” Use only the wording that feels natural; the sentence is a doorway, not a formula.

Everyday examples
  • A careful part checks the message five times so nobody can criticise it.
  • A tired part refuses a project after a striving part has overcommitted.
  • A humorous part changes the subject when a conversation approaches shame.
  • An approval-seeking part agrees before checking whether the person has capacity.

Optional reading: Richard Schwartz, Introduction to Internal Family Systems or No Bad Parts. Book exercises are not automatically course assignments.

Week 3 · The loving gaze of Self

Self as the seat of awareness

Included

The fuller Self teaching behind Week 3, including Guthrie’s metaphors and the distinction between Self and a Self-like part.

Sources: Guthrie Sayen, “Self — ORIGINAL”; old Week 4 handout and live transcript; IFS Institute model outline
Open adapted teaching material
Innate rather than manufactured

In IFS, Self is not earned through correct behaviour and is not destroyed by experience. Access can be obscured when parts carry urgency, fear or responsibility, but the underlying capacity for awareness and relationship remains.

Seat of awareness

Parts are contents of consciousness: a thought, impulse, emotion, image, voice or body pattern. Self is the capacity that can notice those contents, listen to them and hold more than one perspective at once.

Sun and clouds

Guthrie uses the image of the sun: clouds can obscure it without damaging it. The practice is not to attack the clouds but to recognise that the wider light remains present.

Orchestra and conductor

Self can be imagined as a conductor and parts as musicians. Without the conductor there may be no coordination; without the musicians there is no music. Self-leadership values the parts and helps their different abilities contribute to the whole.

Eight Cs and quieter markers

Included

A richer recognition map that prevents “calm” from becoming a pass–fail test.

Sources: Guthrie Sayen, “Self — ORIGINAL”; Richard Schwartz, “Evolution of the IFS Model”; Foundation for Self Leadership 8Cs resources
Open full recognition map
The familiar eight qualities
  • Calm: less internal urgency, not emotional absence.
  • Curiosity: genuine interest rather than investigation with an agenda.
  • Clarity: seeing more of the situation without collapsing complexity.
  • Compassion: recognising suffering without superiority.
  • Confidence: grounded trust that does not need certainty.
  • Courage: willingness to stay present to what matters.
  • Creativity: access to more than the familiar defensive option.
  • Connectedness: contact with body, parts, people and the wider field.
Quieter signals

An easier breath, a softer face, a lower or more open voice, less tunnel vision, more peripheral awareness, reduced attachment to one outcome, or one percent more capacity to listen may all indicate some access to Self.

Signals of a Self-like part

A part can imitate calm while remaining tight, managerial or invested in the “right” result. If there is pressure to forgive, perform compassion or make another part disappear, notice that pressure as another part rather than treating it as failure.

Force and the loving gaze

Teaching anchor

The course’s central relational distinction, translated into observable language.

Sources: Guthrie alignment calls, “BeFriending,” “Self — ORIGINAL,” and live-course language
Open comparison and practice
When force is leading

The inner language is often “get over it,” “calm down,” “explain yourself,” or “change so I can feel better.” Attention narrows and the outcome becomes more important than the relationship.

When more Self is available

The movement may be “I want to understand,” “you do not have to change for me to stay,” or “what would you like me to know?” The part’s pace matters, and “not now” remains a valid answer.

Three-breath experiment
  1. Choose a present, low-intensity part.
  2. Notice how you feel toward it.
  3. If any interest or care is available, let the part receive that quality for three breaths.
  4. If another reaction appears, include it and end without forcing a resolution.

Week 4 · Protectors

Managers and firefighters

Included

A fuller non-clinical map of the two familiar protective styles and what each may contribute when it no longer has to work in an extreme role.

Sources: Guthrie Sayen, “Managers” and “Firefighters”; old Weeks 7–8 handouts, timelines and live transcripts
Open adapted teaching material
Managers: prevention and “never again”

Managers scan the future and try to prevent pain before it arrives. They plan, anticipate, perfect, please, criticise, achieve, control or stay vigilant. Because their work can look responsible and productive, people often identify with managers as simply “who I am.”

A manager may carry a young, parentified logic: “I must keep everything together, and I must do it alone.” In a less extreme role, the same part may offer organisation, discernment, care, standards and the ability to prepare — while also stepping back and resting.

Firefighters: immediate relief

Firefighters respond when pain is already near. Their horizon is the present moment and their rule is often “whatever it takes.” Everyday strategies include scrolling, binge-watching, eating, changing the subject, joking, getting angry, cleaning instead of beginning, working compulsively or going numb.

In a less extreme role, firefighters may bring rest, spontaneity, pleasure, novelty, adventure, humour and the capacity to interrupt a rigid plan.

Same aim, different timing

Managers are Plan A: prevent activation. Firefighters are Plan B: stop or outrun it once it arrives. Both are trying to protect the system, even when their strategies collide.

The protection cycle

Included

A concrete system map that replaces shame about one behaviour with curiosity about the whole sequence.

Sources: Guthrie Sayen, “Managers,” “Firefighters,” and “Exiles”; IFS Institute model outline
Open system map
  1. A manager criticises, controls or pushes in order to prevent failure or rejection.
  2. A vulnerable part feels shame, fear, loneliness or exhaustion.
  3. A firefighter distracts, numbs, escapes or erupts to stop the feeling.
  4. The manager sees the firefighter’s behaviour as dangerous and criticises more intensely.
  5. Both protectors become more extreme because neither trusts that Self can lead.

No single part is the villain. The first useful move is often to recognise the loop and appreciate the intention on both sides before trying to change behaviour.

Use a low-intensity example such as overwork and scrolling. Avoid addiction, self-harm or severe trauma examples in participant practice.

Three messages for protectors

Included

Guthrie’s strong teaching anchor for making gratitude and permission operational rather than sentimental.

Sources: Guthrie Sayen, “Three Messages for Protectors” and “BeFriending”; old Week 5 transcript
Open adapted teaching material
1. Validation

You have a good reason for doing this. I can see the loyalty, work and sacrifice in how you have tried to help.

2. Control and permission

You can decide the pace. You may say no. I will not use appreciation as a trick to get past you.

3. Hope

There may be a future in which you do not have to work this hard and can use your abilities in a role you actually prefer.

Questions that support relationship
  • How are you trying to help?
  • What would you like me to understand?
  • What has this role been like for you?
  • Do you ever get tired?
  • What are you afraid would happen if you stopped?

These messages only work when sincere. A protector’s “no” is information, not resistance to overcome.

Week 5 · Vulnerable parts

Vulnerability inside the system

Included

A humane orientation that explains vulnerability without turning the week into self-directed trauma treatment.

Sources: Guthrie Sayen, “Exiles”; IFS Institute model outline · participant adaptation only
Open adapted teaching material
What IFS calls an exile

IFS uses exile for a young, vulnerable part whose pain, fear, shame or need has been kept out of ordinary awareness because the system believes the feeling would be too much. In this course we often say vulnerable part to keep the first orientation simple.

More than the pain it carries

A vulnerable part is not only a wound. Before carrying a burden, and beneath it, the part may hold openness, trust, play, imagination, sensitivity, tenderness and the capacity for intimacy.

Why protectors gather around it

Managers try to prevent the vulnerable feeling from being activated. Firefighters act when the feeling begins to break through. Respecting those protectors is part of caring for the vulnerable part.

Participant learning goal: recognition and support planning. No searching for origins, witnessing procedure, retrieval or unburdening.

Recognise, orient, choose support

Core safety

The participant-facing response when tenderness appears spontaneously.

Sources: Guthrie Sayen, “How to Handle Exiles”; old-course safety learning; IFS Institute guidance to work with protectors and negotiate pace
Open bounded response
  1. Recognise. Name that vulnerable material may be near without deciding what it is or where it came from.
  2. Stay in the present. Feel the seat, floor and room. Keep the eyes open if that helps.
  3. Include protectors. Notice any wish to move away, go blank, analyse or stop. Thank it and respect its pace.
  4. Choose rather than continue automatically. The next step may be rest, movement, contact with a trusted person, individual therapy, or simply stopping.
  5. Escalate support when needed. Persistent flooding, disorientation, shutdown or risk requires appropriate professional or crisis support.

The aim is not to complete an inner process. It is to remain oriented enough to make a safer choice.

Week 6 · Self-led life

Self-leadership in relationship

Included

A practical relational layer for conflict, intimacy, parenting, leadership and work.

Sources: IFS Institute model outline; Toni Herbine-Blank, “Parts and Self in Relationship”; Richard Schwartz, “You Are the One You’ve Been Waiting For”
Open adapted teaching material
Internal and external systems mirror one another

The way a person treats their own fear, anger or need often shapes how they respond when those qualities appear in someone else. More compassion internally can make another perspective easier to hear without requiring agreement.

Speak for a part, not as the whole truth

“You never listen” can become “A part of me is certain I will not be heard, and it wants to push harder.” The second sentence takes responsibility while keeping the need visible.

Requests and boundaries

A Self-led request can still be firm. The difference is not softness but delivery: less threat, less diagnosis and more clarity about what the speaker feels, needs and will do.

Repair

Parts language does not remove responsibility. A repair can name both the protector and the impact: “A defensive part took over, and I spoke harshly. I understand that it hurt you. I want to return to this differently.”

Stages of familiarity

Adapted frame

A gentle version of Guthrie’s Four Stages of Competence, reframed around recognition rather than practitioner mastery.

Sources: Guthrie Sayen, “Four Stages of Competence”; circles methodology; July 29 continuation discussion
Open developmental frame
  1. I do not yet notice. A part acts and the reaction feels like the whole of me.
  2. I notice afterwards. Reflection reveals the part and the sequence once the moment has passed.
  3. I notice during. Some awareness returns while the reaction is still happening.
  4. Relationship becomes more familiar. Parts can increasingly be noticed within a wider field, without requiring perfect attention.

Development is uneven. A person may have familiar access in one area and little access in another. Mistakes are information, not proof that the practice failed.

Four continuation paths
  • Solo practice for awareness and everyday parts.
  • Community or an integration circle for continuity and shared reflection.
  • Individual therapy when deeper or persistent material needs support.
  • Formal training only when the goal is professional practice.

Research and claims boundary

Authors only

A factual guardrail for course and marketing language.

Source: IFS Institute Research page, accessed August 2026
Open claim guidance

The IFS Institute describes the evidence base as emerging. It notes promising pilot work and reported gains in self-compassion, while also stating that the current literature remains limited and larger replicated trials are needed for specific clinical indications.

  • Describe this course as education and personal development, not treatment.
  • Do not promise trauma healing, recovery from a diagnosis or a guaranteed therapeutic outcome.
  • Keep participant outcomes behavioural and experiential: noticing parts, creating space, using relational language, and recognising support needs.
  • Separate evidence about clinical IFS interventions from claims about this six-week course.

Keep out of this participant week

Practitioner protocols and peer work

Hold back

Six Fs, triads, guiding another person, memory retrieval, exile work, unburdening and practitioner-competence framing are intentionally excluded from this six-week participant course.

Source: old twelve-week archive · internal reference only
Open full rationale
What exists in the old archive

The previous practitioner course included the Six Fs, practitioner–client–observer triads, guiding another person, memory retrieval, exile witnessing, unburdening and questions of practitioner competence or supervision.

Why it is held back here

Moving those materials directly into this course would change both the audience and the level of responsibility. It could overload a foundational participant week and imply peer therapy or practitioner training that this course does not provide.

What may still be translated safely

Some underlying principles can be expressed in participant language: ask permission, appreciate protective intent, respect stop signals, stay with ordinary low-intensity examples, and do not force access to memories or vulnerable material.

The raw practitioner archive remains an internal reference and is not embedded in this public review page.
